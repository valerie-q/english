03
Reading — What Google Says
Natasha Tamiru · Google Workspace · Feb 2024
📄
Part 1 — The big picture
Natasha Tamiru · Google · Feb 2024

Hybrid working has profoundly reshaped our approach to work-life balance and wellbeing. The shift towards flexibility has brought notable benefits, such as increased productivity and access to global talent. But it also presents challenges: mental health, loneliness, and hampered career progression are increasingly familiar issues — and particularly acute for those entering the workforce for the first time.

Having spent over a decade leading organisational transformation programmes for Google's clients, I have seen these implications unfold first-hand. And while the benefits of flexible working are far too great to return to the ways of old, it's become apparent that there's no one-size-fits-all approach available. Instead, we must thoughtfully design our (virtual) workspaces to address the needs of both organisations and the individuals who work for them.

React
The author mentions "hampered career progression" as a downside of remote work. Do you feel this is true in your experience?
She says there's "no one-size-fits-all approach." What would your ideal hybrid policy look like if you were designing it for your team?
📄
Part 2 — Designing for different workspaces

One thing is certain: we shouldn't try to replicate what works face-to-face in an online setting. Back-to-back virtual meetings are much more exhausting than when you're all sitting in the office together, so they should be used more deliberately.

It really helps to think about asynchronous (working independently at one's own pace) and synchronous (working together in real-time) methods — and use both to accommodate different working styles. A real benefit of people working virtually is that they're away from distractions and can do deep work, so it's good to be intentional about how and when to utilise that.

It's often a lack of trust — whether in employees or the process itself — that leads to mandates for full-time office returns and excessive employee monitoring. However, such measures risk alienating talent and jeopardising the psychological safety vital for business effectiveness.

React
The author distinguishes between async and sync work. How does your team currently use each? Is the balance right?
"Psychological safety" — what does that mean to you in a team context? Have you worked somewhere that had it or lacked it?
She says monitoring employees backfires. Do you agree? Have you ever felt monitored too closely?
📄
Part 3 — Trust: cognitive vs affective

At Google, we've made hybrid work by establishing clear rules of engagement — from set office days and core hours, to protocols for video calls. These rules help build two kinds of trust:

Cognitive trust — trust based on job competence, meeting objectives, delivering results.
Affective trust — trust built through stronger interpersonal relationships within teams.

Both are key drivers of psychological safety. Teams and individuals can thrive in any setting — virtual or physical — as long as organisations foster a strong, positive culture led by intentional efforts and shared values. But that won't materialise by itself. It has to be designed for.

Final reaction
Which type of trust — cognitive or affective — do you think is harder to build remotely?
The article ends with "it has to be designed for." Who is responsible for that design — leadership, HR, or each individual team?
After reading all three parts — has your opinion from the beginning of the lesson changed at all?

Grammar Check
🕐
Ex. 1 — Present Perfect or Past Simple?

Choose the correct form. Both options may look possible — think about when and whether it's finished.

  1. Our team switched / has switched to fully remote in 2020.
  2. I worked / have worked from home for three years now.
  3. Did you ever try / Have you ever tried managing a fully remote team?
  4. We lost / have lost two great engineers last quarter because of the return-to-office policy.
  5. She just sent / has just sent the async update, so we don't need a call.
  6. I never met / have never met half of my team in person.
  7. The company introduced / has introduced the hybrid policy when the CEO changed — back in 2022.
Answer key
  1. switched — "in 2020" = specific finished time → Past Simple
  2. have worked — "for three years now" = still happening → Present Perfect
  3. Have you ever tried — "ever" = life experience → Present Perfect
  4. lost — "last quarter" = finished time period → Past Simple
  5. has just sent — "just" = very recently, result relevant now → Present Perfect
  6. have never met — "never" = up to now → Present Perfect
  7. introduced — "back in 2022" = specific finished time → Past Simple

Ex. 4 — Spot the mistake & fix it

Each sentence has one grammar mistake. Find it and correct it.

  1. I am working here since 2019.
  2. She didn't went to the standup yesterday.
  3. If I will have more time, I would learn a new framework.
  4. We have finished the sprint last Friday.
  5. He is more experienced than me, but I work here more longer.
  6. I never met a developer who don't care about code quality.
  7. The team is work remotely since the pandemic.
Answer key
  1. I have worked here since 2019. — since = Present Perfect, not continuous
  2. She didn't go to the standup yesterday. — after didn't, base form
  3. If I had more time, I would learn a new framework. — 2nd conditional: no "will" in if-clause
  4. We finished the sprint last Friday. — "last Friday" = specific time → Past Simple
  5. I work here longer. — no "more" with -er comparatives
  6. I have never met a developer who doesn't care. — Present Perfect + 3rd person -s
  7. The team has been working remotely since the pandemic. — since = Perfect, not simple continuous

Ex. 5 — Rewrite without changing the meaning

Rewrite each sentence using the word in bold. Don't change the meaning.

  1. The last time I worked in an office was in 2021.
    SINCE → I 2021.
  2. It's possible that remote work will become the norm everywhere.
    MIGHT → Remote work
  3. She started learning TypeScript two years ago and she still does it.
    FOR → She two years.
  4. It wasn't necessary for him to come to the office, but he did.
    HAVE → He to the office, but he did.
  5. They built the whole feature without any help from senior devs.
    THEMSELVES → They built the whole feature
  6. My manager always forgets to share the meeting notes.
    NEVER → My manager
Answer key
  1. I haven't worked in an office since 2021.
  2. Remote work might become the norm everywhere.
  3. She has been learning TypeScript for two years.
  4. He didn't have to come to the office, but he did.
  5. They built the whole feature by themselves.
  6. My manager never remembers to share the meeting notes.

Ex. 6 — Same words, different meaning

These pairs look similar but mean something different. Can you explain the difference?

  1. A. I worked at that startup for two years.
    B. I've worked at that startup for two years.
  2. A. If we hire more devs, we'll ship faster.
    B. If we hired more devs, we'd ship faster.
  3. A. She's working on the backend today.
    B. She works on the backend.
Answer key
  1. A = I don't work there anymore (finished). B = I still work there now.
  2. A = realistic plan or real possibility. B = hypothetical — we probably won't, but imagine if we did.
  3. A = just today, temporary. B = her usual role, permanent habit.
