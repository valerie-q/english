Grammar Bank 3B — necessity, obligation and permission

Necessity and obligation

Many phrases can express both necessity and obligation, depending on the context.

I need to take some time off. (It's important — necessity.)
I need to finish this report by tomorrow. (Expected of me — obligation.)

Use be necessary to, be essential to, needs to be done when the focus is on the action:

It's necessary to have a driving licence.
It's essential to take her to hospital immediately.
This job needs to be done by tomorrow morning.

Use have got to and be supposed to when the focus is on the person:

I've got to get home by ten. (informal: I'm gotta)
I'm supposed to wear a hat when I deliver food, but I don't.
Robbie's not supposed to call customers by their first names, but he often does.

Permission

Use be (not) allowed to + infinitive:

Our children are allowed to play computer games for two hours at weekends.
I'm not allowed to go to bed after midnight.

Use be (not) permitted to — more formal:

Office workers are permitted to wear casual clothes on Fridays.
Drivers are not permitted to drive more than eight hours without a break.

Other forms

Most phrases can be used in the future and past with modal verbs:

It will be necessary to take your passport with you.
In my first job it wasn't essential to speak English.
The apartment might need to be painted.

Be supposed to in the past:

We were supposed to go straight home, but we didn't.

Had to for past of have got to:

This morning I had to get up at 4.30 to catch the early train.

Use unless, even if, in case + present tense for present/future; past tenses for hypothetical:

I can't help you unless you tell me the problem.
Even if I won a million euros, I wouldn't change my job.
