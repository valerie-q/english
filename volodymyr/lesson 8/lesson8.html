Grammar recap · from Lesson 7
Say vs. Tell vs. Speak vs. Talk
▾
SAY

Meaning: to express a thought or words — what someone says, without focusing on who they say it to.

Used: quoting direct speech · before a reported clause with no indirect object · with a direct object but no indirect object.

Examples
"Plato is my friend, but truth is a better friend," said Aristotle.
He said he likes horror movies a lot.
Who said that? / Don't say a word.
TELL

Meaning: to inform, instruct, or relate something to a specific person.

Used: with an indirect object (the person being told) · tell + person + to + verb shifts toward "order/instruct".

Examples
Our teacher told us an interesting story.
Tell me everything about yourself!
My father told me to clean up the mess.
Fixed expressions: tell a lie · tell the truth · tell a story · tell tall tales
SPEAK

Meaning: to talk, usually more formal — emphasizes the act of speaking itself, the language used, or addressing a group.

Used: "speak to" / "speak about" · naming a language · giving a speech / addressing an audience.

Examples
She speaks 4 languages.
Today I have spoken to him about a party.
Did Adam speak at the meeting this morning?
Collocations: speak fluently · speak quietly / softly · speak quickly / rapidly
TALK

Meaning: very close to "speak" but more casual/conversational — some situations where "speak" simply wouldn't fit.

Used: mostly informal, everyday conversation — chatting, discussing, casual exchange.

Examples
Please stop talking! ("speak" wouldn't work here)
Did you talk to him yesterday?
My friend talks too much.
"stop talking" — never "stop speaking" in this everyday sense.
Quick comparison
VerbUsed when…Example
sayquoting speech, reported clauses with no indirect objectHe said he likes horror movies.
tellinforming a specific person, giving instructionsMy coach told me what to do.
speakformal register, naming a language, addressing a groupShe speaks four languages.

AUGMENTED REALITY (AR)

Just like VR, AR is also used for a variety of purposes other than gaming. Overlaying virtual objects over reality offers a ton of opportunities businesses can capitalize on. Companies are implementing AR technologies to increase interaction with their brand by offering try-on programs where you can place a virtual object in a real environment — such as a virtual couch in your living room or virtual shoes on your feet. Not to mention special apps where you can hover your phone over a real-life object and learn more information about it. AR events such as AR meetups and concerts is another trend that seems to be taking off.

VIRTUAL REALITY (VR)

We're used to seeing virtual reality in gaming, but does it exist outside it? Yes, it does. There are many fields in which VR technology can be used these days — the most popular ones are education and healthcare. The healthcare industry, in particular, has been a major early adopter of VR. On top of that, VR has applications in business forecasting, allowing businesses to test out new creative products and collect user feedback without needing a finished MVP.

Strong agreement
  • Absolutely! / Totally!
  • 100 percent!
  • I couldn't agree more!
  • That's exactly correct!
Mild agreement
  • I agree up to a point
  • I guess so
  • That might be true in a way
  • That could be true / could happen
Mild disagreement
  • I wouldn't say that myself
  • I don't know about that
  • I'm not so sure, to be honest
  • We'll have to see about that
Strong disagreement
  • Not by a long shot!
  • I don't think that's true
  • That doesn't make sense to me
  • I have to disagree with this
Teacher tip

Strong disagreement can often be interpreted as a sign of rudeness in English-speaking countries. In business communication, it's best to disagree mildly and politely, just to be safe.

Future Tenses — will vs. going to
▾
EX. 1
Spot the grammar

Read the text. Can you name the grammar forms used in the highlighted sentences?

In the next ten to twenty years, specialized and general purpose robots will most likely become both capable and affordable enough to begin replacing many unskilled and semi-skilled workers.

For instance, there has already been invented a robotic burger maker that can make up to 360 burgers per hour. In the future, fast-food restaurants will need less space for the kitchen (which will save them in rent and utilities, etc.) since these machines only take up a mere 24 square feet.

The robots don't "walk off the job" or need training. So there will not be so much unpredictability and wasted time.

For instance, Panera Bread, a fast-casual chain, is going to replace some workers with kiosks. There will be roughly eight checkout kiosks per restaurant to help solve the slow-service issue that they currently have.

Then there's Amazon.com. They currently use 1,000 robots from a company they bought back in 2012 called Kiva Systems. They use these robots to help fill customer orders. And, according to the latest report, Amazon is going to have up to 10,000 robots working by the end of this year!

Answer key

will most likely become / will need / will save / will not be — Future Simple (predictions based on general belief, no hard evidence).
is going to replace / is going to have — to be going to (predictions based on existing evidence — Panera's announced kiosk rollout, Amazon's published robot count).

EX. 2A
Look at the timelines and complete the rules
"I'll..." — decision made now
"I'm going to..." — decision made earlier
  1. We use for decisions made at the moment of speaking, and promises. — "I need another coffee." "No worries, I'll make you a cup."
  2. We use when we have already made a decision and are now talking about it. — "Josh and I have decided to organize a team retreat. We're going to invite everyone to a ski resort."
  3. Both are used to make predictions: with hard evidence, we use ; based on feelings or intuition, we use .
Answer key
  1. Future Simple (will)
  2. to be going to
  3. evidence → to be going to; feelings/intuition → will
EX. 2B
Forms and usage
Future SimpleTo be going to
AffirmativeI will be here until 7 o'clock.I'm going to talk about three main points today.
QuestionsWill she know what to do?Are you going to take a break soon?
NegationsI won't ever go back there.He isn't going to quit his job.
We usually contract "will not" to "won't", in both speech and writing. "Will not" (in full) is mostly used in very formal communication (contracts, formal agreements) or to show anger/strong emotion — "I will NOT work with him again!"
